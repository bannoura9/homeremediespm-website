How many unrelated people can legally live in a Boulder rental?

This is the single most expensive assumption an out-of-town owner can make in Boulder: that a house may be rented to as many people as it has bedrooms.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 9, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • Boulder limits the number of unrelated people who may occupy a dwelling. The limit depends on zoning district and property, not bedroom count.
  • The rules have been revised over time — verify the current limit for your specific address with the City of Boulder.
  • Enforcement is largely neighbor-driven, and the city acts on complaints.
  • Over-occupancy is generally the owner's compliance problem even when tenants created it.
  • Your defenses are a specific lease occupancy clause and regular inspections.

Boulder has restricted unrelated occupancy for a long time. The policy is contested, has been amended, and remains one of the defining features of owning rental property in the city.

The practical version: the number of unrelated adults who may lawfully share a dwelling is set by ordinance and varies by zoning district and property. It is not determined by how many bedrooms the house has, how large it is, or what the previous owner was doing.

Why owners get this wrong

  • They assume bedrooms equal tenants. A five-bedroom house near campus looks like a five-student property. It may not be one.
  • They rely on what the seller said. "It's always been rented to four students" describes a practice, not a permission — and possibly describes a violation.
  • They rely on outdated information. Boulder has amended these rules; a forum post or an article from a few years ago may describe a regime that no longer applies.
  • They do not notice drift. A lawful three-tenant house becomes an unlawful five-tenant house when two partners move in over a year. Nobody announced it.

How enforcement actually happens

Boulder does not patrol for over-occupancy. It responds to complaints, and complaints come from neighbors.

The triggers are consistent: cars. Too many vehicles on the street outside one house is the most common signal, followed by noise, trash volume, and simply the number of people visibly coming and going.

That means the practical risk is a function of your relationship with the street. A property whose neighbors have a responsive manager's phone number is a property whose neighbors call the manager. A property with an absent owner is one where the next call goes to the city.

It is your problem even when it is not your fault

Owners find this unfair and it is nonetheless the position. If tenants add occupants beyond the limit, the compliance exposure generally attaches to the property and the owner.

"I didn't know" is not a defense, largely because the city's view is that an owner who does not know is an owner who was not looking.

The two things that actually protect you

1. A specific lease occupancy clause. Name the permitted occupants individually. State the maximum. Define how long a guest may stay before becoming an occupant — a defined number of nights, not "reasonable." Require written approval to add anyone. Make a breach a lease violation you can act on.

Without this, you have no contractual route to address over-occupancy even after you discover it. See our lease requirements guide.

2. Regular inspections. With proper notice, at least twice a year, more for student properties. You are not looking for anything dramatic — you are counting toothbrushes, beds, cars and shoes at the door. Drift is visible to anyone who walks through the property and invisible to everyone who does not.

Before you buy

If a Boulder purchase depends on a particular occupancy assumption, verify it in writing during due diligence. Call the City of Boulder, describe the specific address and zoning, and ask what the limit is. Get it in writing or note who you spoke to and when.

Then build the pro-forma on that number. A property that only works at five tenants and is lawful at three is not a property that works.

What about family?

The restrictions target unrelated occupancy, so a single family is treated differently from a group of unrelated adults. The precise definition matters and has been the subject of amendment, so if your situation sits near the boundary — an extended family, a couple with roommates, a multi-generational household — check the current definition rather than assuming.

Fair housing considerations also apply to familial status, which is a separate body of law from local occupancy rules. Where the two interact, get advice. See our screening guide for the fair housing basics.
