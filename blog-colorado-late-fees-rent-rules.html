Late fees in Colorado: the limits, the grace period, and the trap

Late fees are the most-copied and least-checked clause in any lease. Colorado has restricted them significantly, and a fee schedule inherited from a national template is very likely wrong.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 9, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • Colorado requires a grace period before a residential late fee may be charged.
  • Late fees are capped, both as a flat amount and as a proportion of monthly rent.
  • You generally may not evict solely for unpaid late fees, and may not treat a late fee as rent for eviction purposes.
  • Charging a non-compliant fee can carry penalties well beyond simply refunding it.
  • Late fees are a poor collection tool anyway. Consistency and early contact recover more money.

Late fee clauses are almost never written from scratch. They get inherited — from a template, a previous owner, or another landlord's lease. Which is why so many Colorado leases contain fee schedules that were never lawful here.

Colorado has tightened this area considerably. If your lease says something like "$50 if rent is not received by the 1st, plus $10 per day thereafter," it is worth having reviewed. That structure has problems on at least three fronts.

The grace period

Colorado requires a grace period after rent is due before a late fee may be charged on a residential tenancy. A fee charged on the second of the month, before the grace period has run, is not enforceable — and charging it may expose you to more than the fee is worth.

This is the most common defect. Owners set the fee to trigger immediately because that seems like the strongest deterrent. In Colorado it is simply not permitted.

The cap

Colorado limits how large a residential late fee may be, framed both as a flat maximum and as a percentage of the monthly rent, with the lower governing. Daily accruing fees that compound past that ceiling are the second common defect — a $10 per day fee reaches an unlawful level quickly on an ordinary rent.

Because the specific figures are set by statute and have been amended, confirm the current limits rather than working from a number you remember. This is exactly the sort of provision that gets revised.

Late fees are not rent

This distinction matters more than it sounds.

Colorado restricts treating unpaid late fees as rent for eviction purposes, and generally does not permit eviction solely for unpaid late fees. An owner who serves a demand for possession that lumps together unpaid rent and accumulated late fees may have served a defective notice — and a defective notice means starting over.

Similarly, applying a rent payment to late fees first and then declaring the rent short is the kind of move that creates a much larger problem than the fee. Keep rent and fees in separate columns, always.

What a non-compliant fee can cost

The exposure is not simply refunding an over-charge. Colorado provides remedies for improperly charged fees that can substantially exceed the amount collected, and a lease containing an unlawful fee provision invites scrutiny of everything else in it.

An owner collecting an unlawful $75 late fee from twelve tenants over three years has not made $2,700. They have created a liability considerably larger than that, sitting quietly until someone notices.

What actually improves collection

The uncomfortable truth is that late fees do not work well as a collection tool. A tenant who can pay usually pays on time regardless of the fee. A tenant who cannot pay is not motivated by a penalty they also cannot pay.

What genuinely improves on-time payment:

  • Automatic payment set up at move-in, before it is ever an issue.
  • Contact on day one of lateness — a short, neutral message. Most late rent is an oversight, and most oversights resolve within a day if someone mentions them.
  • Absolute consistency. A fee waived for one tenant and enforced against another is both a fair housing risk and an announcement that the policy is negotiable.
  • Screening. Payment history is the strongest available predictor. See our tenant screening guide.
  • Acting quickly when it is real. A tenant two months behind rarely catches up. Late fees delay the decision without changing it.

What to do now

Read your own late fee clause. Check the grace period, check the amount against the current statutory cap, check that it does not accrue daily past the ceiling, and check that nothing in your lease treats late fees as rent. If any of those is wrong, fix it at renewal and stop charging under it in the meantime.

Frequently asked questions

How much can a landlord charge for late rent in Colorado?

Colorado caps residential late fees, expressed both as a flat maximum and as a percentage of monthly rent, with the lower controlling. The figures are set by statute and have been amended, so verify the current limits rather than relying on a remembered number.

Is there a grace period for rent in Colorado?

Yes. Colorado requires a grace period after rent is due before a residential late fee may be charged. A fee triggered immediately on the day after rent is due is not enforceable.

Can I evict a tenant for unpaid late fees in Colorado?

Generally no. Colorado restricts treating unpaid late fees as rent for eviction purposes and does not permit eviction solely for unpaid late fees. Combining rent and late fees in a demand for possession risks a defective notice.

Can I apply a rent payment to late fees first?

This is risky in Colorado and can create a much larger problem than the fee is worth, including a defective notice if you then treat the rent as short. Keep rent and fees accounted for separately.

What happens if my lease has an illegal late fee clause?

The exposure generally exceeds refunding the overcharge — Colorado provides remedies for improperly charged fees, and an unlawful clause invites scrutiny of the rest of the lease. Stop charging under it and correct the lease at renewal.
