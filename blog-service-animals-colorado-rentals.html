Fair housing

Service animals and emotional support animals: what a Colorado landlord may and may not do

This is the area where well-meaning landlords generate the most fair housing liability. Not through hostility — through applying a pet policy to something that is not a pet.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 9, 2026·7 min read

The short answer

  • Assistance animals are not pets. No pet fee, no pet deposit, no pet rent, no breed or weight restriction.
  • A "no pets" policy does not apply to a service animal or a properly documented emotional support animal.
  • You may ask limited questions when the disability or the need is not obvious. You may not ask for a diagnosis or medical records.
  • The tenant remains liable for actual damage the animal causes. That never went away.
  • Denials are possible but narrow — a specific animal posing a direct threat or causing substantial property damage, assessed individually.

Almost every fair housing complaint about animals starts the same way. A landlord with a reasonable, evenly applied no-pets policy receives a request involving an animal, applies the policy, and finds out afterwards that the policy did not apply.

The mistake is categorical, not attitudinal. Under fair housing law, an assistance animal is not a pet. It is an accommodation. Different rules, different analysis, different consequences for getting it wrong.

The two categories

Service animals are dogs (and in some contexts miniature horses) individually trained to perform tasks for a person with a disability — guiding, alerting, retrieving, interrupting a behavior. The training is the defining feature.

Emotional support animals, or assistance animals more broadly, provide therapeutic benefit through their presence. No specific training is required and they are not limited to dogs.

In housing, both are treated as reasonable accommodations rather than pets. The distinction matters more in public accommodation law than in a rental context — for a landlord, the practical rules are largely the same.

What you may not do

  • Charge a pet fee, pet deposit or pet rent. This is the most common violation and the easiest one to lose.
  • Apply breed, size or weight restrictions. A breed restriction in your insurance policy is a conversation with your insurer, not a reason to deny an accommodation.
  • Refuse because of a no-pets policy. The policy does not reach assistance animals.
  • Demand medical records, a diagnosis, or details of the disability.
  • Require specific certification, registration or an ID card. There is no legitimate national registry, and the websites selling certificates have no legal standing.
  • Insist the animal wear a vest or demonstrate its training.
  • Treat the request as a reason to scrutinize the applicant differently in other respects. This is retaliation, and it is visible in the record.

What you may do

If the disability is not obvious and the need for the animal is not obvious, you may request reliable documentation that the person has a disability and that the animal provides disability-related assistance. That is the scope of it — a confirmation of need, not a medical file.

You may also:

  • Hold the tenant responsible for actual damage the animal causes, deducted from the standard security deposit like any other damage.
  • Require the animal be under control and not create a nuisance.
  • Require compliance with local licensing, leash and vaccination rules.
  • Conduct an individualized assessment if a specific animal has a documented history of dangerous behavior.

The narrow grounds for denial

Accommodation requests are not unconditional. You may deny where:

  • The specific animal poses a direct threat to health or safety that cannot be reduced by another reasonable accommodation, or
  • The specific animal would cause substantial physical damage to property, or
  • The accommodation would impose an undue financial and administrative burden, or fundamentally alter your operations.

Every one of these requires an individualized assessment of that animal, based on objective evidence of actual conduct. "Pit bulls are dangerous" is not an individualized assessment. "This dog bit a previous neighbor, here is the animal control report" is.

Before denying anything on these grounds, talk to an attorney. The cost of the call is trivial against the cost of a fair housing complaint.

Fraudulent requests

Landlords raise this constantly, and the frustration is legitimate — the online certificate industry has made genuine requests harder to distinguish. But the calculation is lopsided. Wrongly denying a legitimate accommodation costs vastly more than wrongly granting a marginal one.

Handle it procedurally rather than adversarially. Have a written accommodation process. Apply it identically to everyone. Request documentation within the permitted scope when the need is not obvious. Document what you asked, what you received, and when you decided. Then decide.

A consistent, documented process is the defense. Suspicion is not.

Frequently asked questions

Can I charge a pet deposit for an emotional support animal?

No. Assistance animals, including emotional support animals, are not pets under fair housing law. You may not charge a pet fee, pet deposit or pet rent for one. You may still hold the tenant liable for actual damage the animal causes.

Can I ask for proof that an animal is an emotional support animal?

Where the disability and the need are not obvious, you may request reliable documentation confirming that the person has a disability and that the animal provides disability-related assistance. You may not request a diagnosis, medical records, or details of the condition.

Do online ESA registrations and certificates count?

There is no legitimate national registry for assistance animals, and certificates purchased online carry no legal weight either way. Their absence is not grounds for denial, and their presence is not conclusive proof.

Can I refuse an assistance animal because of my insurance policy?

A breed restriction in your insurance policy is generally a matter to take up with your insurer, not a basis for denying a reasonable accommodation. Discuss the situation with an attorney and your carrier before refusing on this ground.

Does a no-pets policy apply to service animals?

No. A no-pets policy does not reach service animals or properly documented assistance animals, because they are not pets under fair housing law. Applying the policy to them is one of the most common fair housing violations landlords commit.
