Short-term rentals in Boulder County: the rules that actually bind

Short-term rental rules across Boulder County are considerably stricter than owners expect, and the most common restriction — a primary-residence requirement — rules out investment properties entirely.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 22, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • Most Boulder County jurisdictions require a separate short-term rental license, distinct from any long-term rental license.
  • Primary-residence requirements are common, which excludes pure investment properties.
  • HOAs prohibit short-term rentals almost universally, independently of what the city allows.
  • Lodging taxes apply and must be registered for and remitted.
  • Operating unlicensed carries fines that accumulate, and platforms increasingly verify license numbers.

The short-term rental pitch is appealing: higher nightly rates, flexibility, and a market that clearly exists in a place people want to visit. The regulatory reality in Boulder County is much less accommodating.

The four independent checks

Every one of these has to permit it. Any single failure ends the plan.

1. The city or county. Nearly every jurisdiction here requires a specific short-term rental license, separate from any long-term rental license. A residential rental license does not authorize short-term use.

2. The primary-residence requirement. This is the big one. Many Front Range jurisdictions, including Boulder and Denver, restrict short-term rental licenses to a property that is the operator's primary residence. If you do not live there, you generally cannot license it. That single rule eliminates the pure-investment short-term rental model.

3. The HOA. Associations prohibit short-term rentals almost universally now, typically through minimum lease terms of 30 days or more. City permission is irrelevant if the covenants forbid it. See our HOA guide.

4. Taxes. Short-term rentals attract lodging or accommodations taxes on top of ordinary sales tax obligations, which must be registered for and remitted. Platforms collect some of this; do not assume they collect all of it.

Why the rules are this tight

Boulder County has a genuine housing shortage, and short-term conversion removes long-term housing stock. The policy response has been consistent across jurisdictions and has tightened over time rather than loosened. It is reasonable to plan on the basis that restrictions will get stricter, not looser.

What happens if you do it anyway

  • Fines that accumulate, often per day of violation.
  • Platform delisting. Airbnb and Vrbo increasingly require and verify license numbers in regulated jurisdictions.
  • HOA enforcement — fines, liens, and injunctive relief.
  • Insurance problems. A standard landlord policy generally does not cover short-term rental operation. An undisclosed short-term use can void coverage exactly when you need it. See our insurance guide.
  • Tax liability for unremitted lodging taxes, with penalties.

The insurance point is the one owners underweight. A denied claim on an uninsured loss is far larger than any fine.

The mid-term alternative

Where short-term is prohibited, mid-term rentals — furnished, 30 days or longer — sometimes sit outside the short-term regime while still earning above unfurnished long-term rates.

Boulder County has genuine demand for this: visiting faculty, researchers on fixed appointments at the federal labs, travelling medical staff, and families between homes. It is a real niche here in a way it is not everywhere.

But verify before assuming. Check the jurisdiction's definition of short-term, the HOA's minimum lease term, whether furnished mid-term use triggers lodging tax, and whether your insurance covers it. The answers vary by city and the boundary is not always where you would expect.

The honest comparison

Owners comparing short-term against long-term usually compare gross nightly revenue against monthly rent, which flatters short-term considerably. The real comparison includes cleaning between stays, furnishing and replacement, platform fees, higher utility costs, lodging taxes, much higher management fees, vacancy between bookings, and a genuinely different workload.

In Boulder County the question is usually moot, because the primary-residence rule or the HOA settles it. Where it is genuinely open, run the comparison honestly before committing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I run an Airbnb in Boulder?

Only in limited circumstances. Boulder requires a short-term rental license and restricts it to a property that is the operator's primary residence, which excludes pure investment properties. An HOA may prohibit it independently. Verify current rules with the city before proceeding.

Does a rental license cover short-term rentals?

No. A residential rental license authorizes long-term tenancies. Short-term use is governed by a separate licensing regime with its own requirements, usually including a primary-residence rule and lodging tax registration.

Can my HOA stop me from doing short-term rentals?

Almost certainly, and most now do — typically through a minimum lease term of 30 days or more. HOA covenants apply independently of what the city permits, so city approval does not override them.

What are mid-term rentals?

Furnished rentals of roughly 30 days or longer, which sometimes fall outside the short-term regime while earning above unfurnished long-term rates. Boulder County has real demand from visiting faculty, lab researchers and travelling medical staff. Verify the jurisdiction's definitions, HOA minimum terms, tax treatment and insurance coverage before assuming it is permitted.

What happens if I operate an unlicensed short-term rental?

Accumulating fines, platform delisting where license numbers are verified, HOA enforcement including liens, unremitted lodging tax liability with penalties, and potentially voided insurance coverage. The insurance exposure is usually the largest and the most underweighted.
