Renting out a home in Boulder: the license, the inspection and the occupancy rules

Boulder has required rental licenses since long before the rest of the metro caught on, and it enforces them properly. If you own a rental in Boulder, licensing is not optional and the occupancy rules are stricter than you expect.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 10, 2026·7 min read

The short answer

  • Boulder requires a rental license for residential rentals, and has done so for decades. Enforcement is active.
  • Licensing includes an inspection against minimum housing standards, with periodic re-inspection on renewal.
  • Boulder has occupancy limits restricting how many unrelated people may share a dwelling. Over-occupancy is the classic out-of-town-owner mistake.
  • A local contact who can respond quickly is generally expected — a problem if you live out of state.
  • Verify current requirements with the City of Boulder before applying. The rules are revised more often than most cities'.

Boulder is the hardest rental market in Colorado to operate in casually. That is not a criticism of the city — it is a consequence of a large student population, a constrained housing supply and decades of policy built around both. The result is a regulatory environment that assumes landlords will be professional about it.

If you own a Boulder rental and have been treating it like a rental in, say, Firestone, there are three things to check.

1. The license

Boulder requires a rental license for residential rental property. This is long-established, not a recent addition, and the city does enforce it. Licensing is tied to an inspection against minimum housing standards, and licenses have to be renewed on a cycle — with re-inspection.

The inspection covers the usual life-safety ground: alarms, heat, electrical safety, egress, structural and moisture condition. Boulder's older housing stock and the volume of finished basements in the city mean egress and ventilation issues come up frequently.

2. Occupancy limits

This is the one that surprises owners from outside Boulder, and it is the most expensive mistake available.

Boulder restricts the number of unrelated people who may occupy a single dwelling unit. The specifics depend on the zoning district and the property, and the city has revised the rules over time — but the principle has held for a long time. A four-bedroom house is not automatically a house you may rent to four unrelated students.

Owners get caught two ways. Some rent to a group over the limit knowingly, gambling that nobody will report it. Others rent to a legal number of tenants who then quietly add roommates. The second is more common and it is still the owner's problem. Over-occupancy complaints in Boulder are frequently made by neighbors, and they get acted on.

If you are buying a Boulder rental on the assumption you can fill every bedroom, verify the occupancy limit for that specific property before you close, not after.

3. A local contact who can actually respond

Boulder expects a rental to have a responsible local point of contact — someone who can be reached and can act. Combined with Colorado's habitability response timelines, this is a genuine practical problem for an owner who has moved away and is managing remotely.

A relative who agrees to be listed but does not answer the phone is not a solution. Neither is a voicemail box.

Property management in Boulder

What Boulder gets you in return

It is worth saying plainly: Boulder is a demanding place to be a landlord and a very good place to own rental property. Vacancy is low, demand is structural rather than cyclical, and rents reflect it. The regulation is the price of a market where finding a tenant is rarely the hard part.

The owners who do badly in Boulder are not the ones who follow the rules. They are the ones who treat it as a passive investment, discover the licensing and occupancy requirements after a complaint, and spend the next six months catching up.

Elsewhere in Boulder County

Requirements vary considerably between Boulder, Longmont, Louisville, Lafayette, Superior and Erie. Do not assume a rule from one applies in the next. Our city-by-city overview is the place to start.

Frequently asked questions

Does Boulder require a rental license for a single-family home?

Yes. Boulder's rental licensing requirement covers residential rental property broadly, including single-family homes. It is not limited to apartment buildings or student housing.

How many unrelated people can live in a Boulder rental?

Boulder limits occupancy by unrelated persons, with the specific limit depending on zoning and the property. The city has revised these rules over time, so check the current limit for your specific address with the City of Boulder rather than relying on what a previous owner or agent told you.

What happens if my Boulder tenants add extra roommates?

Over-occupancy is generally the owner's compliance problem even when the tenants created it. This is why occupancy language in the lease matters, and why someone needs to actually notice. Regular inspections are the practical defense.

Do I need a Boulder rental license if I only rent part of the year?

Very likely yes, and short-term rentals have separate and stricter requirements including a primary-residence rule. Confirm which category applies before advertising the property.

Can I manage a Boulder rental from out of state?

Legally you may own it, but Boulder expects a responsive local contact, and Colorado's habitability response timelines are short. Remote management of a Boulder property without someone local is a practical risk, not just an administrative one.
