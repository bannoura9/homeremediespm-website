Pets

Should you allow pets? And how to screen them properly

Roughly two-thirds of American households have a pet, and a no-pets policy excludes most of your applicant pool to avoid a risk that is smaller and more manageable than owners assume.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 23, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • Banning pets removes most of your applicant pool and typically lengthens vacancy.
  • Pet rent generally beats a one-off pet deposit — it accrues for the whole tenancy and is not refundable.
  • Assistance animals are not pets. None of this applies to them, and charging a pet fee for one is a fair housing violation.
  • Screen the animal, not just the species — age, weight, training, vet records, prior rental history.
  • Require renters insurance with animal liability included.

The no-pets policy is usually inherited rather than decided. Someone had a bad experience once, or the previous owner did, and it became the rule.

Run the arithmetic and it rarely survives.

The case for allowing pets

  • A far larger applicant pool. Most households have a pet. Excluding them means competing for a minority of the market and waiting longer for it.
  • Pet-friendly properties lease faster. Fewer competing listings accept animals, so yours stands out.
  • Longer tenancies. Pet owners find it harder to move, because their next landlord may say no. That is worth real money — see what turnover costs.
  • Additional income through pet rent.

Against that: some risk of damage, some risk of odor, and occasional noise complaints. Real, but manageable — and cheaper than three extra weeks of vacancy on most properties.

Pet rent versus pet deposit

Most owners default to a one-time refundable pet deposit. Monthly pet rent generally serves you better:

  • It accrues across the whole tenancy rather than being a single sum.
  • It is not refundable, so it is genuine income rather than money you are holding.
  • It scales with how long the animal is in the property, which is roughly how wear works.

Note that a refundable pet deposit is generally treated as part of the security deposit and falls under Colorado's deposit rules — including the return deadline and treble damages for willful retention. Non-refundable pet fees are treated differently and the distinction matters, so have a Colorado attorney confirm how your lease characterizes each.

You may still deduct for actual pet damage from the security deposit in the normal way, provided you documented condition at move-in. See our inspection guide.

The category that is not a pet

Service animals and emotional support animals are assistance animals, not pets. None of this section applies to them.

You may not charge pet rent, a pet deposit or a pet fee for an assistance animal. You may not apply breed or weight restrictions. A no-pets policy does not reach them. Charging a pet fee for an assistance animal is one of the most common fair housing violations landlords commit, and one of the easiest to lose.

Read service animals and ESAs in Colorado rentals before you handle a request. Handle assistance animals under a separate lease provision from your pet policy — a single clause covering both is where the trouble starts.

Screening the animal

Ask for the same rigor you apply to the tenant:

  • Species, breed, age and weight. Age matters more than breed — a settled seven-year-old dog is a lower risk than a puppy of any breed.
  • Spayed or neutered, and house-trained.
  • Vaccination and vet records, plus the vet's contact details.
  • A reference from a previous landlord specifically about the animal. This is the single most useful item and almost nobody asks for it.
  • Any bite history or complaints.
  • A photograph. It sounds trivial; it tells you whether the "small dog" is a small dog.

Set a limit on the number of animals, and put the approved animals in the lease by name and description so an approved cat does not quietly become three.

Insurance

Two things to check. Your own landlord policy may carry breed exclusions — know what they are before you approve an animal, and speak to your carrier rather than assuming.

And require the tenant to carry renters insurance with animal liability included, naming you as an additional interest so you learn if it lapses. A dog bite claim against an uninsured tenant becomes your problem quickly.

Practical protections

  • Hard flooring in high-traffic areas at the next turnover. It solves most pet damage permanently.
  • Document condition thoroughly at move-in, including carpet and any odor.
  • Inspect during the tenancy, with notice, twice a year.
  • Address complaints promptly — barking is the most common neighbor issue and it escalates if ignored.

The honest position: a well-screened pet in a documented, inspected property is a small risk that pays for itself. An unscreened animal in a property nobody visits is a genuine one. The difference is process, not policy.

Frequently asked questions

Should I allow pets in my Colorado rental?

Usually yes. Most households have a pet, so excluding them shrinks your applicant pool substantially and typically lengthens vacancy. Pet-friendly properties also tend to lease faster and hold tenants longer, which matters more than the damage risk on most properties.

Is pet rent better than a pet deposit?

Generally yes. Pet rent accrues across the whole tenancy, is not refundable, and scales roughly with how wear actually accumulates. A refundable pet deposit is usually treated as part of the security deposit and falls under Colorado's deposit rules, including the return deadline and treble damages exposure.

Can I charge pet rent for an emotional support animal?

No. Assistance animals, including emotional support animals, are not pets under fair housing law. You may not charge pet rent, a pet deposit or a pet fee, and you may not apply breed or weight restrictions. You may still hold the tenant liable for actual damage.

What should I ask when screening a pet?

Species, breed, age, weight, whether spayed or neutered and house-trained, vaccination and vet records, any bite history, and a photograph. The most useful item is a reference from a previous landlord about that specific animal, which almost nobody requests.

Can my insurance stop me allowing certain dog breeds?

Landlord policies sometimes carry breed exclusions. Check yours and speak to your carrier before approving an animal. Note that insurance exclusions are not a basis for refusing an assistance animal — that is a separate conversation with the insurer.
