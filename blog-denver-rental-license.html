The Denver residential rental license, explained for owners

Denver phased in a citywide residential rental license requirement that now reaches essentially every rented home in the city, including single-family houses and condos. Plenty of owners still do not know it applies to them.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 10, 2026·7 min read

The short answer

  • Denver requires a residential rental license for long-term rentals — including a single house or condo, not just apartment buildings.
  • A third-party inspection by an approved inspector is part of the process, and it is where most first-time applicants get delayed.
  • Renting without a license is a code violation and can carry fines. It can also complicate an eviction.
  • Licenses are renewable, not permanent. Diary the expiry date the day you receive it.
  • The rules and fees are set by the City and County of Denver and do change — verify current requirements on denvergov.org before you apply.

Denver's residential rental license requirement was rolled out in stages, starting with larger multi-unit properties and eventually extending to every long-term residential rental in the city. That phased approach is exactly why so many owners missed it. If you bought a rental in Denver before the requirement reached your property type, nothing arrived in the mail to tell you the rules had changed underneath you.

The short version: if you rent a residential property in Denver for longer than 30 days at a time, you almost certainly need a license. That includes a single condo. It includes the house you moved out of and decided to rent rather than sell. It includes properties you inherited.

What the license actually involves

There are three parts, and the middle one is the one that catches people.

  • An application through the city's licensing system, with ownership details and a local point of contact.
  • A property inspection by a third-party inspector on the city's approved list. You hire and pay the inspector directly; the city does not send one.
  • A fee, which varies by the number of units and by license type.

The inspection is a minimum-housing-standards check, not a full home inspection. It is looking at life-safety and habitability basics — working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, functioning heat, safe electrical, no obvious structural or moisture problems, proper egress from sleeping rooms. It is not looking at cosmetic condition or appliance age.

Why owners get delayed

Almost always for the same handful of reasons:

  • Egress windows in basement bedrooms. A finished basement room that has been rented as a bedroom for years may not have a conforming egress window. This is the single most expensive failure on the list, and it is common in Denver's older housing stock.
  • Missing or wrongly placed alarms. Carbon monoxide detectors in particular — the placement requirements are specific and a plug-in unit in the wrong hallway will not pass.
  • Handrails and guardrails. Older stairs frequently do not meet current spacing requirements.
  • Waiting until the last minute. Approved inspectors get booked, and a failed inspection means repairs plus a re-inspection. Starting the process two weeks before a tenant moves in is not enough time.

What happens if you rent without one

Operating an unlicensed rental in Denver is a code violation and can carry fines that accumulate. That is the direct cost. The indirect cost is worse: an unlicensed rental can complicate your position if you ever need to enforce the lease or pursue an eviction, and it is exactly the kind of detail a tenant's attorney will find.

It also tends to surface at the worst moment — during a sale, during a refinance, or when a neighbor complains.

Renewals are the part everyone forgets

The license is not permanent. It has a term and it has to be renewed, and the renewal may require a fresh inspection depending on the license type and how much time has passed. The first license is the one owners remember; the renewal three or four years later is the one that lapses quietly.

Put the expiry date in a calendar the day the license is issued, with a reminder set several months ahead. That is not sophisticated advice, but it is the difference between a routine renewal and a violation notice.

Denver is not the only city with rules

Licensing and rental-registration requirements vary enormously across the metro. Some cities require a license for every rental, some only for certain property types, and some have no requirement at all. Our overview of rental license requirements by city covers the north metro and Boulder County.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Denver rental license for a single condo?

Almost certainly yes. Denver's residential rental license requirement applies to long-term residential rentals generally, not only to large multi-unit buildings. A single condo or house rented for more than 30 days at a time falls within it. Verify your specific situation with the City and County of Denver.

How long does it take to get a Denver rental license?

Plan for several weeks rather than several days. The application itself is quick, but you have to schedule a third-party inspection, and if anything fails you need time for repairs and a re-inspection. Starting at least six to eight weeks before you need the property tenanted is realistic.

Who pays for the rental inspection?

The owner. You hire an inspector from the city's approved list directly and pay them; the city does not provide the inspector. Costs vary between inspectors, so it is worth getting more than one quote.

What is the most common reason a Denver rental inspection fails?

Non-conforming egress windows in basement bedrooms, followed by missing or incorrectly placed smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. Both are common in Denver's older housing stock and neither is obvious to an owner who has never been through the process.

Does an Airbnb need a residential rental license?

Short-term rentals in Denver are governed by a separate short-term rental license with its own rules, including a primary-residence requirement. The residential rental license discussed here applies to long-term tenancies. Check which category your use falls into before applying.
