Longmont in 2026: Boulder County demand at a different price

Longmont is the most underrated rental market in Boulder County. It draws on much of the same regional employment as Boulder at a substantially lower entry price, which is exactly the combination that produces yield.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 9, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • Longmont's entry prices are well below Boulder's while rents hold up — the yield gap is the whole thesis.
  • Demand comes from people who work in Boulder County but cannot or will not pay Boulder prices.
  • The tenant base skews toward families and long-term working households, which means longer tenancies.
  • Regulation is lighter than Boulder's, though you should still verify current city requirements.
  • Longer tenancies and lower turnover matter more to returns than headline rent. See what turnover costs.

Boulder gets the attention. Longmont gets the returns.

The two markets share a labor shed — a substantial number of Longmont residents work in Boulder, Louisville, Superior, Broomfield or along the northwest corridor. What differs is the price of entry, and it differs a great deal.

What drives Longmont demand

  • Boulder County employment at a reachable price. The single largest driver. People who work in the county and cannot justify Boulder housing costs.
  • Its own employer base — Longmont has genuine local employment in manufacturing, technology and healthcare rather than being purely a bedroom community.
  • Access. The Diagonal to Boulder, I-25 to the north metro and Denver, and the northwest corridor.
  • An actual downtown. Main Street has been meaningfully revitalized, and that shows up in rental demand.

Who rents here

This is where Longmont differs most usefully from Boulder. The tenant base skews toward working families and established households rather than students and short-stay professionals.

The practical consequences are all favorable to an owner:

  • Longer tenancies. Families move less than students and postdocs.
  • Lower turnover cost. Turnover is the largest controllable cost in rental ownership — see the arithmetic.
  • Different wear profile. More ordinary use, less annual make-ready.
  • Predictable leasing calendar. Family moves cluster around the school year rather than the university one, which is a gentler cycle to work with.

The yield case

The argument is straightforward. A property in Longmont costs materially less than a comparable property in Boulder, while rent does not fall by the same proportion. That gap is the return.

Boulder's advantage is structural scarcity and appreciation. Longmont's is cash flow and tenancy stability. An owner optimizing for monthly numbers should be looking at Longmont, and many looking at Boulder have not run the comparison.

A realistic figure based on comparable homes that actually leased — not a guess from a listing site.

Longmont property management

What to watch

  • Verify city requirements. Longmont's licensing and registration position is lighter than Boulder's, but confirm the current rules for your address rather than assuming. See our city-by-city overview.
  • Know the neighborhood. Longmont is not uniform. Old Town, the newer eastern subdivisions and the areas near the employment corridors all behave differently.
  • Flood history. The 2013 flood shaped parts of Longmont significantly. Check floodplain status and insurance implications during due diligence.
  • Older housing stock in the core. Charming, and with the maintenance and licensing-inspection profile that age implies.
  • HOAs in newer subdivisions frequently restrict rentals — check the covenants before committing. See our HOA guide.

Longmont, Firestone and Frederick

Immediately north and east, Firestone and Frederick offer newer construction at lower entry prices again, with lower maintenance burdens and a commuter tenant base. Worth looking at alongside Longmont rather than instead of it — they are a different trade between yield, age and location.

The summary

Longmont is the practical answer for an owner who wants Boulder County exposure and functioning cash flow. It will not appreciate like Boulder and it does not carry Boulder's regulatory load. For most owners, that trade is favorable.

Frequently asked questions

Is Longmont a good place to buy rental property?

For yield, it is among the strongest options in Boulder County. Entry prices sit well below Boulder while rents hold up, and the tenant base skews toward families with longer tenancies. Boulder still has the edge on appreciation and structural scarcity.

Why is Longmont cheaper than Boulder?

Longmont is not inside Boulder's growth boundary and has been able to add housing, so supply is not constrained the same way. It draws on much of the same regional employment without the same scarcity premium.

Who rents in Longmont?

Predominantly working households and families, including many people employed elsewhere in Boulder County. That produces longer tenancies and lower turnover than a student-driven market.

Does Longmont require a rental license?

Longmont's requirements are lighter than Boulder's, but municipal rules change and vary by property type. Verify the current position for your specific address with the City of Longmont rather than relying on general guidance.

Longmont or Firestone and Frederick?

Firestone and Frederick offer newer construction at lower entry prices with lighter maintenance, drawing on a commuter tenant base. Longmont has more local employment, an established downtown and a deeper rental market. It is a trade between yield, building age and location rather than a clear ranking.
