Rent

Getting rent paid on time, every month

Rent collection is a system, not a personality trait. Landlords who collect reliably are not tougher than the ones who do not — they have removed the friction and they act on day one.

By Nadir Maaiah·August 9, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • Automatic online payment set up at move-in is the single highest-impact change available.
  • Contact on day one of lateness. Most late rent is an oversight and resolves immediately.
  • Be careful with partial payments — accepting one can affect a pending notice. Get advice before you do.
  • Never accept cash. No record, and a dispute you cannot win.
  • Consistency is a legal requirement, not just good practice. Selective enforcement is a fair housing risk.

Every landlord has a theory about being firm or being understanding. Neither predicts who gets paid. What predicts it is whether paying is effortless and whether lateness gets noticed immediately.

Remove the friction

Set up automatic payment at move-in. Not "let them know the options" — set it up while they are signing, before it is ever a question.

Online payment through a portal or ACH means rent moves without anyone remembering to do anything. Most late rent is not financial distress; it is a person who meant to pay and did not get to it. Automation removes that category entirely.

Never accept cash. No record, no proof of amount or date, and a dispute you will lose. If a tenant has no bank account, use money orders or a cashier's check — and give a written receipt every time.

Set the terms clearly in the lease

  • The exact due date
  • The grace period — Colorado requires one before a late fee may be charged
  • The late fee, within Colorado's statutory caps
  • Accepted payment methods, and that cash is not one
  • Where partial payments stand
  • What happens when rent goes unpaid

Colorado restricts residential late fees significantly, both in amount and in when they may be charged, and a fee schedule copied from a national template is very likely non-compliant. See our late fee guide — a non-compliant fee can cost far more than it collects.

Day one

This is the whole method.

The day rent is late, send a short, neutral message. Not a threat, not a lecture — a note that it has not arrived, and a question about whether something went wrong.

A large share of late rent resolves within twenty-four hours of someone mentioning it. The card expired, the transfer failed, the payment was scheduled for the wrong month. Nobody is hiding.

Landlords who wait a week to say anything are the ones with chronic problems, because silence teaches that the due date is approximate.

Partial payments — get advice before accepting

A tenant offers half the rent. Instinct says take it; something is better than nothing.

Be careful. Accepting a partial payment can affect the status of a demand for possession you have served or intend to serve, and in some circumstances requires starting the notice process again. It can also muddy your position on what is owed.

If you are considering enforcement, ask a Colorado attorney how a partial payment interacts with your notice before you accept it. If you do accept one, document in writing exactly what it covers and that it does not waive anything.

Payment plans

For a good long-term tenant with a genuine one-off problem, a written payment plan is often the commercially sensible answer. A tenant who catches up is worth far more than a vacancy plus an eviction.

Put it in writing: the amount owed, the catch-up schedule, that ongoing rent must still be paid in full and on time, and what happens if the plan is broken. A verbal arrangement is a dispute waiting to happen.

Offer this once. A tenant needing a second plan is usually telling you the tenancy is not sustainable.

When to stop waiting

The hardest judgment, and owners consistently wait too long.

A tenant one month behind may catch up. A tenant two months behind rarely does — the gap compounds faster than most household budgets can absorb. Every additional month is rent you will probably never collect, on top of the eviction cost.

Being decisive here is not harshness. Letting arrears build helps nobody: the tenant accumulates a debt and a judgment they cannot clear, and you lose money the whole way. Acting early gives both sides a cleaner outcome.

When it reaches that point, follow the process exactly. See our guides to Colorado eviction notices and the eviction process. Do not combine unpaid rent and late fees in a demand for possession — that can produce a defective notice.

Consistency is not optional

Apply the same rules to every tenant. Waiving a fee for one and enforcing it against another is a fair housing risk, and it announces that the policy is negotiable.

If you want discretion, build it into the policy itself — for example, one fee waiver per tenancy, available to everyone — rather than exercising it case by case.

It starts with screening

Payment history is the strongest available predictor of payment behavior. Verify income properly, contact previous landlords, and ask specifically whether rent arrived on time. See our screening guide — collection problems are usually screening problems that arrived late.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to collect rent as a landlord?

Automatic online payment or ACH, set up at move-in before it becomes a question. Most late rent is an oversight rather than distress, and automation removes that category. Never accept cash — there is no record and any dispute becomes unwinnable.

Should I accept a partial rent payment?

Be careful. Accepting a partial payment can affect the status of a demand for possession and may require restarting the notice process. If you are considering enforcement, ask a Colorado attorney how it interacts with your notice before accepting, and document exactly what any payment covers.

When should I start the eviction process?

Owners typically wait too long. A tenant one month behind may catch up; a tenant two months behind rarely does, because the gap compounds faster than most budgets absorb. Acting early produces a cleaner outcome for both sides than letting arrears build.

Can I charge a late fee the day after rent is due?

No. Colorado requires a grace period before a residential late fee may be charged, and caps the amount. A fee schedule copied from a national template is very likely non-compliant, and a non-compliant fee can cost considerably more than it collects.

Should I offer a payment plan?

For a good long-term tenant with a genuine one-off problem, usually yes — a tenant who catches up is worth far more than a vacancy plus an eviction. Put it in writing, require ongoing rent to be paid in full alongside the catch-up, and offer it once.
