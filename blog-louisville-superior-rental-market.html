The Louisville and Superior rental market

Louisville and Superior sit between Boulder and the north metro, and they consistently appear near the top of national rankings for small-town quality of life. The Marshall Fire changed both, and the effects are still working through the housing stock.

By Nadir Maaiah, Home Remedies Property Management · Westminster, CO·August 9, 2026 · updated October 19, 2026·6 min read

The short answer

  • The Marshall Fire rebuild is now largely complete: ~96.7% of destroyed Louisville homes rebuilt or underway, and permits cover ~74% of Superior's losses, over half already occupied.
  • A typical one-bedroom runs roughly $1,900–$1,930 and a two-bedroom $2,200–$2,375 in both towns — note the wide range, partly driven by newer rebuilt homes entering the rental pool at a premium.
  • Both towns combine strong schools, Boulder County access and easy commutes in every direction.
  • The tenant base is professional households and families — stable, long tenancies.
  • Much of Superior sits in HOA communities with rental restrictions. Check covenants before buying.
  • Entry prices sit below Boulder and above Longmont, with yields to match.

Louisville and Superior are easy to describe and hard to buy into. Excellent schools, quick access to Boulder, Denver and the tech corridor, well-maintained neighborhoods, and a quality of life that shows up in national rankings year after year.

They are also two of the communities most affected by the Marshall Fire, and any honest picture of the rental market here has to start there.

The rebuild is largely done — here's where it actually stands

The December 2021 Marshall Fire destroyed a large number of homes across Superior, Louisville and unincorporated Boulder County. The immediate effect on rentals was severe and obvious: hundreds of displaced households needed somewhere to live in exactly the area where housing was already tight.

As of mid-2026, that's no longer the active story. In Louisville, roughly 96.7% of destroyed homes have been rebuilt or are underway. In Superior, permits now cover about 74% of destroyed buildings, and more than half of those are already cleared for occupancy. Unincorporated Boulder County is the exception — still only around 34% rebuilt — but inside the two towns themselves, this is functionally a finished recovery, not an ongoing one.

For owners the implications are practical:

  • The displaced-household rental demand has normalized. That temporary surge is behind both markets now.
  • New construction has replaced older stock across both towns — newer homes, current building standards, better efficiency, and noticeably higher rents for the rebuilt units specifically.
  • Insurance is still a live issue, rebuild or not. Premiums and availability across the wildland-urban interface remain tighter than pre-2021. Get a quote before you buy, not after. See our landlord insurance guide.

That last point deserves weight. Insurance cost is a material line in any Front Range pro-forma, and in this corridor specifically it can change whether a deal works — rebuild status doesn't change that.

Where rents stand right now

As of October 2026, a typical one-bedroom runs roughly $1,900 to $1,930 in either town, and a two-bedroom $2,200 to $2,375. Treat the top of that range with some caution — newer rebuilt homes are pulling some listings well above $2,500 for a one-bedroom and near $4,900 for a two-bedroom, which skews published averages upward. A comp-based pricing check on your specific property matters more here than in most of our coverage area, precisely because the housing stock is now a genuine mix of pre-fire and post-fire construction sitting side by side.

Who rents in Louisville and Superior

Professional households and families, predominantly. People working in Boulder, Broomfield, the northwest tech corridor or Denver, who want the schools and are willing to pay for them.

This produces the tenant profile owners generally want: stable employment, long tenancies, properties treated well. It also means demand is tied to the school calendar rather than the university one — leasing clusters around summer for a school-year start.

The HOA question, especially in Superior

A large share of Superior's housing sits in planned communities with active homeowners associations, and many restrict rentals — caps on the number of rented units, minimum lease terms, waiting periods after purchase.

Check the recorded covenants before buying anything you intend to rent, and confirm current availability if there is a cap rather than just whether renting is permitted in principle. This catches buyers out regularly and it is entirely avoidable. See our HOA restrictions guide.

Where these towns sit on price

Entry prices generally fall below Boulder and above Longmont, with yields in the same relative position. Compared with Erie and Lafayette next door, you are paying more for less new construction, but buying into finished neighborhoods rather than a multi-year supply wave still working its way through the pipeline. You are paying for schools, location and stability rather than for scarcity.

The trade is reasonable. Louisville and Superior will not deliver Longmont's cash flow or Boulder's structural scarcity, but they offer very low vacancy risk and a tenant base that stays. Given that turnover is the largest controllable cost in rental ownership, tenancy length is worth more than the headline yield suggests.

Practical notes

  • Get insurance quoted during due diligence, not after closing.
  • Read the HOA covenants before committing, particularly in Superior.
  • Verify city rental requirements — see our city-by-city overview.
  • Align lease dates with the school calendar, not the university one.
  • Newer builds mean lower maintenance in the rebuild areas — a genuine advantage worth pricing in.

Frequently asked questions

How did the Marshall Fire affect the rental market?

It destroyed a large number of homes across Superior, Louisville and unincorporated Boulder County, displacing hundreds of households into an already tight rental market. As of mid-2026 the rebuild is largely complete in the two towns: about 96.7% of destroyed Louisville homes are rebuilt or underway, and permits cover roughly 74% of destroyed Superior buildings, with more than half of those already cleared for occupancy. Unincorporated Boulder County lags well behind at around 34%.

Is insurance harder to get in Superior and Louisville now?

Premiums and availability across the Front Range wildland-urban interface have tightened considerably. Insurance cost is now a material line in any pro-forma for this corridor. Get a quote during due diligence rather than after closing.

Can I rent out a house in Superior?

Often yes, but a large share of Superior housing sits in HOA communities that restrict rentals through caps, minimum lease terms or waiting periods. Read the recorded covenants before buying, and confirm current cap availability rather than just whether renting is allowed in principle.

Who rents in Louisville and Superior?

Predominantly professional households and families working in Boulder, Broomfield, the northwest tech corridor or Denver, drawn by the schools. Tenancies tend to be long and properties well treated, with leasing clustered around the school calendar.

Are Louisville and Superior good rental investments?

They offer very low vacancy risk and unusually stable tenancies, at entry prices below Boulder and above Longmont. Yields sit in between accordingly. Given that turnover is the largest controllable cost in rental ownership, the tenancy stability is worth more than the headline yield suggests.
